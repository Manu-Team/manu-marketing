# Solo Indie Founder Bio Positioning (Press Outreach Reference)

> Filed: 2026-06-01. Tier: WARM (press kit material).
> Scope: Boilerplate founder bios + voice/positioning guidance for Tideward (ship 2027-02-11).
> Repo context: `Manu-Team/manu-marketing` is a PUBLIC GitHub repo. Anything here is web-discoverable the moment it's pushed. No private contacts, no salary numbers, no draft-stage backstory.

## TL;DR

Press doesn't read founder bios for biography. They read them to answer one question: **"Is there a story here, and can I tell it in one sentence?"** The best bios for solo indie devs (Pope, Barone, Wreden, LocalThunk, Malcolm) all share the same architecture: **one specific credibility anchor + one personal reason this game exists + nothing else**. The "lifelong gamer" opener is dead. Listing every job since college is dead. AAA-pedigree claims work *only when true and recent*; the dominant 2026 mode is the **cross-domain pivot** (Eric Barone the unemployed CS grad; LocalThunk the IT worker; Malcolm the self-taught hobbyist) framed without apology.

For Seth and Tideward, the load-bearing recommendation is: **lead with the game and its lineage, then place the founder as the person who needed this game to exist, then mention the SEO background only when journalists ask.** The pivot story is interesting, but it's a B-side. The A-side is "solo dev, multi-Apple-platform, Melvor-lineage idle RPG, Feb 11 2027."

## What Press Actually Looks For

Jade King, Lead Features Editor at TheGamer, summarized the 2026 inbox in one line: *"Journalists do not want essays; they want clarity."* (IndieGameBusiness, "The Essential Honest Guide to Working with Games Media in 2026.") Across the same guide and Acorn Games, GameDev.net, IMPRESS, and Campaign Cooperative coverage, the actual pitch-read protocol is:

1. **Subject line + first sentence.** Decides whether the email gets opened at all.
2. **The "X meets Y" comparison.** Tells the editor whether their audience is in.
3. **Visual proof.** Screenshots, GIFs, or trailer thumbnail loadable in the email client.
4. **One-sentence dev hook.** The role the founder bio plays. This is the part that decides whether the piece is a *news brief* (no bio needed) or a *feature* (bio becomes the spine).

The **5-second hook** is the working unit. It is a single line that gives the journalist the angle they can pitch to their own editor. The line that wins is *specific* — "ex-Naughty Dog tool programmer who moved to rural Japan and spent personal savings on a passport-stamping sim" beats "veteran developer with 8 years of AAA experience makes indie game." Specificity buys credibility; generic claims burn it.

What press explicitly does not want, per the same sources: cold DMs through personal social, essay-length pitches, "I've been a gamer since I was 5" openers, ALL CAPS, vague claims of innovation, and any pitch that assumes coverage is owed.

## The Three Bio Lengths

The press-kit-industry-standards research already locked these as the three required lengths. Each has a different job.

### 50 words — the social caption / podcast intro
Pure hook + name + game + platform + release. No backstory. Every word load-bearing. The test: a podcast host can read it on-air without skipping or paraphrasing. If they paraphrase, you wrote it wrong.

### 150 words — the news brief
Hook + two or three credibility sentences + one-line elevator pitch + release info. The credibility sentences are where the bio earns its keep. Pick the *one* anchor that is most specific and most relevant to this game; ignore everything else from the resume. The test: a news editor can paste it into a 200-word brief with two minor edits.

### 500 words — the feature-piece skeleton
Full arc with backstory, motivation, design philosophy. This is the version a long-form profile writer will quote *from* (not paste). It is the only version where the personal story matters. Even here, leave at least one open thread — a reason for the writer to email back and ask for a quote. A 500-word bio that answers every possible question gets used as a press release, not a feature. A 500-word bio that leaves one door open (why this game, why now, why solo) gets interviewed.

## Case Studies

### 1. Toby Fox (Undertale, Deltarune)
**Anchor:** Composer-turned-developer who did EarthBound ROM hacks before commercial release.
**What works:** Specificity of the ROM-hack lineage anchors him to a niche audience that already trusts that pedigree. He never claimed industry experience he didn't have.
**What's missing on purpose:** No business framing, no "team of one" boasting, no design philosophy statements. He let the game do that work.
**Lesson:** Toby's bio strategy is the *most extreme version of "show, don't tell"* a solo dev can run. Works only when the game itself is loud enough to carry it. Most first-time devs cannot afford this restraint.

### 2. Lucas Pope (Papers, Please / Return of the Obra Dinn)
**Anchor:** Ex-Naughty Dog tool programmer on Uncharted 1 & 2.
**What works:** The Naughty Dog credit is short, specific, and time-bounded (he left around 2010). It buys massive credibility without making him sound like he's coasting on it. The follow-on detail — moved to rural Japan with his wife to make smaller things, funded Papers, Please from personal savings — is the personal hook.
**What's missing on purpose:** No claim to be a designer at Naughty Dog (he wasn't; he was a tool programmer). The bio is honest about the role.
**Lesson:** AAA pedigree is gold if you describe the actual role accurately. Inflated AAA claims are a credibility-killer; honest ones are the strongest single credential a solo dev can carry.

### 3. Davey Wreden (The Stanley Parable / The Beginner's Guide)
**Anchor:** USC critical-studies degree; self-taught Source Engine modder.
**What works:** The combination signals *intellectual frame* (critical studies) and *learn-by-doing* (self-taught modder). Both serve the kind of games he makes. He doesn't hide that Stanley Parable started as a Half-Life 2 mod.
**What's missing on purpose:** No industry resume because there isn't one.
**Lesson:** First-timers with no industry resume can build a credible bio out of *the specific path that led to this specific game*. The path is the credential.

### 4. LocalThunk (Balatro)
**Anchor:** Anonymous former IT worker from Saskatchewan who built it during pandemic vacation.
**What works:** The anonymity itself became the story. The few details he did release (Big Two card-shedding game with friends, IT day job, no poker player) are weirdly specific in a way that disarms skepticism.
**What's missing on purpose:** His real name, photos, location below province level, day-job employer.
**Lesson:** Pseudonymity is *only* a workable strategy when the game's hook is strong enough to carry the void where a founder narrative would normally sit. Do not borrow this strategy unless your game is a phenomenon already.

### 5. Brendan Malcolm / Games by Malcs (Melvor Idle)
**Anchor:** Self-taught indie developer from Perth, Australia; began Melvor Idle in 2018 as a way to fit a RuneScape-flavored game around an adult schedule.
**What works:** The "fit it around adult life" frame is concrete and relatable. It explains *why this is an idle game* without ever using the word "design philosophy." It also signals to the journalist that the founder is the target audience for the game — a fact that lands well in profile pieces.
**What's missing on purpose:** No credentials drama. He calls himself self-taught, names the city, and moves on.
**Lesson:** This is the closest template to what Tideward should run. Practical, personal, geographically specific, design-rationale buried inside a life-stage fact.

### 6. Matías Colotto (Magic Research 1 & 2)
**Anchor:** Solo developer, Argentina, idle RPG with a tight UI-driven scope.
**What works:** Very little visible bio at all. Colotto let the games and the Reddit-driven distribution story carry his profile. Coverage that does exist describes him through his work rhythm and his market discipline, not his backstory.
**Lesson:** For a deeply systems-driven solo dev in the idle/incremental niche, a low-volume bio is viable. The audience and press for this genre cares more about the game's depth than the founder's narrative arc. Tideward sits in this exact lane.

### 7. Eric Barone / ConcernedApe (Stardew Valley)
**Anchor:** UW Tacoma CS grad, couldn't find an industry job, worked as a Paramount Theatre usher in Seattle, built Stardew because Harvest Moon disappointed him. Girlfriend Amber Hageman worked two jobs to support him.
**What works:** Every sentence is a *specific* hardship-and-motivation fact. None of it is bragging. The unemployment, the usher job, the partner's support are all surface-level facts that a journalist can confirm in 30 seconds and that humanize the story without sympathy-mining.
**What's missing on purpose:** No "I always knew" framing. No design-philosophy statements until well into the project's life.
**Lesson:** When the backstory has built-in stakes, name them plainly. Don't dramatize; the facts dramatize themselves.

### 8. Daniel Mullins (Pony Island / The Hex / Inscryption)
**Anchor:** CS undergrad who made paper games as a kid, moved through Flash and RPG Maker to XNA/Unity, worked a few years at Vancouver studios, then shipped Pony Island as his first commercial indie.
**What works:** The tools progression (paper → Flash → RPG Maker → XNA → Unity) is a credibility ladder. The Vancouver studio years are mentioned briefly without naming employers — enough to anchor "he's a real programmer," not enough to be coasting on a brand.
**Lesson:** Tool-and-engine progression can substitute for a famous studio credit. Journalists understand what it signals.

### 9. Cliffski / Cliff Harris (Positech)
**Anchor:** Ex-Lionhead Studios programmer who left to run a one-person company making strategy/sim games (Democracy, Production Line, Gratuitous Space Battles).
**What works:** "Ex-Lionhead" is one short clause that buys credibility; the rest of the bio is about the kind of games he makes and the indie-economics philosophy he writes about publicly.
**Lesson:** A single short clause of pedigree, then move on. Don't list every credit.

### 10. Vampire Survivors / Luca Galante (poncle)
**Anchor:** Self-described "TV programmer" (not games industry) who built Vampire Survivors as a side project. The game's massive success became the bio.
**Lesson:** The "outsider with a non-games tech career" frame is *very* live in 2026 press. It signals authenticity and disarms the "another bedroom dev pitching a derivative roguelike" reflex.

### Common pattern across all 10
Every single one of these bios names a *specific* anchor — a studio, a degree, a job, a city, a relationship, a game they grew up with. None of them lead with "passionate about games" or "lifelong gamer." None of them claim to be visionaries. Several actively under-claim. The under-claim is the move.

## Voice Spectrum

There are three working lanes for solo-dev voice. Pick one and stay in it across all surfaces.

| Lane | Practitioners | When it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-effacing / understated** | LocalThunk, Pope, Wreden, Barone | When the game is the loud part. Default for systems-heavy games where the founder is closer to a craftsman than a personality. |
| **Visionary / philosophical** | Jonathan Blow, Patrice Désilets | Only credible when the founder already has a major shipped title AND public writing or talks to back the philosophy. Disastrous for first-timers. |
| **Practical / craftsman** | Brendan Malcolm, Colotto, Cliffski | When the founder talks about the game like an engineer talks about a build — fit-around-adult-life, tight scope, replayability. Best fit for the idle/incremental niche where Tideward lives. |

For Tideward, the lane is **practical/craftsman with a thin coat of self-effacement**. Malcolm's voice is the closest reference. No visionary talk. No "lifelong gamer." Plain-stated facts about scope, lineage, and platform commitment.

## Credibility Hooks That Work

In rough order of strength for a first-time indie dev:

1. **Honest AAA / studio credit, named accurately.** Pope's "tool programmer at Naughty Dog on Uncharted 1 & 2" beats "8 years at a major studio." Specificity wins.
2. **Specific cross-domain expertise that maps to the game's craft.** Stardew's farming research; Papers, Please's UI tooling; Inscryption's prior CS work. The map between past skill and current game has to be visible in one sentence.
3. **Years of related-domain practice, named with a number.** "Eight years of consulting in X" lands. "Veteran" doesn't.
4. **Personal stake that explains why this game exists.** Malcolm's "fit around adult life," Barone's Harvest Moon disappointment. The reason this game was built by *this* person.
5. **Geographic specificity.** Perth, Saskatchewan, rural Japan. Costs nothing, anchors the bio in a real human.
6. **Education only if relevant or recent.** Wreden's USC critical studies maps to The Beginner's Guide. Barone's CS degree from UW Tacoma maps to Stardew. A 15-year-old MBA does not.

The strongest single hook a first-timer can carry is **a specific cross-domain expertise + a personal reason this game exists**. Two short clauses. That is enough.

---

## Credibility Hooks That Backfire

- **"Lifelong gamer" / "I've been playing games since I was 5."** Says nothing. Every founder in the inbox can say it.
- **"Built games since I was 12 in Flash."** Reads juvenile unless the bio is also showing a current professional output. By itself, signals hobbyist.
- **Listing every job since college.** Resume, not bio. Press eyes glaze.
- **"Visionary" / "innovative" / "revolutionary" applied to self.** Read as marketing-AI red flag in 2026.
- **Vague AAA claims.** "Worked at major studios" without names triggers skepticism. Either name them or skip.
- **Manifesto paragraphs about what's wrong with the industry.** Powerful when Jonathan Blow does it. Disastrous for everyone else.
- **Cookie-cutter "passionate about creating immersive experiences" filler.** Identifies a press release as AI-assisted or junior-PR-assisted on sight.
- **Sympathy-mining hardship framing.** State the facts plainly. Don't editorialize the difficulty. Barone's bio works because the facts are stark; if he had written *about* the struggle, it would land worse.
- **Long lists of inspirations.** "Inspired by Hollow Knight, Celeste, Stardew Valley, Undertale, Outer Wilds, Tunic, and Inscryption" reads as derivative. Pick one or two, name them sharp.

## Cross-Industry Pivot Angles

Seth's specific case: SEO consulting → idle RPG dev. Three possible framings, with honest tradeoffs.

### Option A — Lead with the pivot
"From optimizing search rankings to optimizing skill curves." Pros: novel, memorable, gives press a clean hook. Cons: SEO has a perception problem in 2026 (post-AEO/GEO churn, public skepticism of the discipline). The pivot framing can read as "tech dabbler tries games," which is exactly the frame that closes journalist inboxes.

### Option B — Bury the pivot, lead with the game and the audience
"Seth is a solo developer in North Carolina who has spent two years on Tideward, a multi-Apple-platform idle almanac in the lineage of Melvor Idle." The SEO background is on the personal site or LinkedIn for any journalist who wants it, but it isn't the headline. Pros: avoids the dabbler frame; lets the game speak first; respects the lane (idle/incremental press cares about systems, not pivots). Cons: less colorful; loses a possible feature-piece hook.

### Option C — Use the pivot only in the 500-word version, not the 50/150
The 50- and 150-word bios stay clean; the 500-word version has one sentence on the cross-domain skill ("years optimizing systems users return to weekly") that maps to the discipline of designing an idle game. Pros: best of both. Cons: requires discipline in interviews not to lead with the pivot when asked.

**Recommendation:** Run Option C. The pivot is a genuine feature-piece angle but it is the wrong A-side. The 50/150 are about the game; the 500 lets the journalist discover the pivot if they want it.

A note on the audience-map data from the X-positioning research: the systems-loving incremental-game audience cares about *the game's depth* far more than the founder's career arc. They are not the audience for a personality-led founder narrative. Lean into the practical-craftsman voice and let the pivot live in long-form pieces.

## Tideward / Seth Recommendation

### 50-word bio (canonical)
Tideward is the debut game from Seth Reichelt, a solo developer in North Carolina. An idle almanac in the Melvor Idle lineage, Tideward ships February 11, 2027 across iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple TV, Apple Vision Pro, and Apple Watch — every Apple platform in one purchase.

*(48 words. Includes the most underused hook in the kit: every-Apple-platform-in-one-purchase. That is the unique 5-second story.)*

### 50-word bio (alternate, lineage-led)
Two years in the making, Tideward is a Melvor-lineage idle RPG built by Seth Reichelt, a solo developer in North Carolina. Launches February 11, 2027 — natively on iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple TV, Vision Pro, and Apple Watch as a single universal purchase.

### 50-word bio (alternate, design-first)
Tideward is an idle almanac you can carry across every Apple device. Built solo by Seth Reichelt in North Carolina over two years, it ships February 11, 2027 — one purchase covers iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple TV, Apple Vision Pro, and Apple Watch.

### 150-word bio
Tideward is a solo-built idle almanac in the lineage of Melvor Idle, shipping February 11, 2027 across every Apple platform — iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple TV, Apple Vision Pro, and Apple Watch — as a single universal purchase. The developer, Seth Reichelt, is a solo founder based in western North Carolina with eight years of background in search and systems consulting before turning to game development in 2024. Tideward grew out of a simple question: what would an idle RPG look like if it respected adult schedules and treated cross-device continuity as a first-class design constraint? Two years and one full Apple-ecosystem build later, Tideward is the answer. The game is structured as a years-long, depth-first almanac of interlocking skills, with weekly recurring leaderboards through Game Center and design-led commitments to no ads and no engagement-bait monetization.

*(147 words.)*

### 500-word bio
Tideward is the debut game from Manu Games LLC, a single-member studio run by Seth Reichelt out of western North Carolina. The game ships February 11, 2027 as a universal purchase across every Apple platform — iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple TV, Apple Vision Pro, and Apple Watch — making it one of the small handful of indie titles in 2027 to treat the full Apple device family as one product rather than six SKUs.

Tideward is an idle almanac in the lineage of Melvor Idle: a depth-first, systems-heavy RPG built for players who want a game they can return to over years, not finish in a weekend. The design constraints were set early. No ads. No engagement-bait monetization. No mandatory online play. A weekly leaderboard cadence through Game Center so the competitive layer respects the fact that idle games are paced to real life, not to push notifications. Saves move with players across devices through iCloud, so the same character lives on whichever screen happens to be closest.

Reichelt is a first-time game developer, but not a first-time builder of systems users return to. Before founding Manu Games in 2024, he spent eight years consulting on search and discoverability for early-stage companies — a discipline whose core question (what makes someone come back next week?) turns out to map cleanly onto idle RPG design. Tideward inherited that lens. Every skill, every loop, and every reward in the game was tuned against a question Reichelt asked thousands of times in his previous work: is there a reason to be here again tomorrow?

The studio is named after the original codename of the project, Manu, which remains visible across the source repositories and research library. The user-facing brand became Tideward in May 2026 after a trademark search and a long internal conversation about what the game actually felt like. The retained codename across the codebase is intentional. It is a quiet acknowledgment that game development is the slow accumulation of small decisions, most of which the audience never sees.

Manu Games is a private single-member LLC. The studio has no outside investors, no publisher, and no employees. Tideward was built entirely on personal savings and a deliberately small scope. It is not a "vision" or a "movement." It is a game one person made because no existing game scratched the specific itch — an idle RPG with the depth of Melvor, the cross-device respect of Apple's best first-party apps, and the long-game pacing of an almanac you keep on a shelf.

Press inquiries: press@tideward.app. Press kit: [tideward.app/press](https://tideward.app/press). The studio replies to all genuine outreach within five business days; review codes are available from Steam Next Fest 2026 onward.

*(498 words.)*

### Interview cheat-sheet (what to say when asked about background)

- **If asked about the SEO/consulting background:** "Eight years optimizing things people come back to every week. Idle RPGs are the same question with different inputs." One sentence. Move on.
- **If asked why a first-time dev built for every Apple platform:** "It was the same code path. The hard part was deciding to be disciplined about it, not the platform work." Honest, technical, not boastful.
- **If asked about the team:** "It's one person. No employees. No outside funding." Don't elaborate unnecessarily.
- **If asked about lineage / Melvor:** Name it plainly. Credit. Don't position Tideward as "better than Melvor"; position it as "in the lineage of."
- **If asked about the rebrand from Manu Idle to Tideward:** Brief, factual, no drama. "Trademark cleared, the new name felt right, we kept the codename internal."
- **If asked about monetization:** Lead with what it isn't (no ads, no engagement bait). Then what it is.
- **If a journalist tries to make it a "solo dev triumph" story before launch:** Decline the frame politely. The triumph story is post-launch. Pre-launch, it's a craftsman's story.

## What to Leave Out

- **Salary numbers, revenue projections, savings figures.** Public repo. Never.
- **Apple Small Business Program 15% commission.** Internal economics; not the story.
- **Failed prior projects** unless thematically load-bearing. Tideward has none worth mentioning; do not invent retrospective hardship.
- **Religion, politics, controversy** of any kind. Including industry controversy.
- **Any home address detail or city-level location.** "Western North Carolina" or "North Carolina" only. The PMB is on the corporate side; never the bio side.
- **The codename / brand history,** unless asked. Keep it ready for the 500-word and interview, not the 50/150.
- **Specific names of previous SEO consulting clients.** Public repo, NDAs, attribution risk.
- **"Visionary," "passionate," "revolutionary," "game-changer," "innovative."** Each one chips credibility.
- **Em dashes in the social-distributed version of any bio.** Per the project's AI-voice-tell convention (commas / colons / bullets only in external copy).
- **Any wording that begs to be screenshotted out of context.** Strong opinions go in interviews where context is preserved, not in the bio where it isn't.

## Update Cadence

Revise the founder bio at four trigger points:

1. **New shipped product or major milestone.** Tideward 1.0 launch (2027-02-11) is the first major rewrite. The 50-word becomes "developer of Tideward, available now on every Apple platform." The 150-word adds the launch reception sentence once there's a real one. The 500-word gets a "what changed since launch" coda.
2. **Sales / milestone crossed worth naming.** First 100K, first 1M downloads, first major award nomination. Update only with a verifiable, specific number. Round numbers and "huge success" framings get cut.
3. **Press placement worth quoting.** Once Tideward has two or three reviews scoring 8+ (or equivalent in the genre's review economy), the 500-word version gets one short pull-quote in the credibility block.
4. **Annual refresh.** Every twelve months, regardless of milestones. Press kits go stale. The dates, the studio age, the "two years in the making" become "now in its third year" and so on. Calendar this for the first Monday of February each year.

**Versioning convention:** the file `marketing/boilerplate/founder-bio.md` should carry a frontmatter `version: YYYY-MM-DD` line at the top, plus a "previous versions" footer linking to the git history. Every bio update is its own commit with the rationale in the message. Never rewrite in place without a commit history; press will quote the version that was live at the time of their reporting, and the trail matters if anything is ever disputed.
